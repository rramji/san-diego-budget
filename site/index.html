San Diego's budget gap, explained

The City of San Diego plans to spend about $6.5 billion this budget year (FY2027, July 2026 to June 2027). Only about $2.3 billion of it is general-purpose money that the City Council decides how to use (the General Fund). That part has not added up on its own for years. Here is how long, where the money goes, what closing the gap would cost you, and what the numbers say about five common claims.

How long has this been going on?

Since FY2012, the city's regular yearly costs have often been bigger than its regular yearly income (a structural deficit). The difference was covered with money that will not come back, such as federal COVID relief, savings, and skipped deposits into the city's reserve fund (one-time resources). Each bar shows that money as counted by the Council's own budget office, the Independent Budget Analyst (IBA); years the IBA did not report are hatched, not zero. The gap peaked at about $181M in FY2025, and the IBA expects about $30M in FY2028, according to KPBS.

The bars are not all measured the same way. Through FY2021, each bar counts all the one-time money in that year's proposed budget. From FY2022, each bar is what was left after subtracting one-time spending, in the adopted budget. The FY2027 bar comes from the IBA's review of the proposed budget.

Where is the money, and what can actually be cut?

Most of the city's money is spoken for before anyone votes. Of the the city plans to spend, only is general-purpose money that the City Council decides how to use (the General Fund). The rest is tied by law to specific uses, such as water service or road projects. Even inside the General Fund, the yearly pension payment (the pension ADC) and debt payments are set by contract, so the bars below narrow the budget to the part that can be cut.

What would closing the gap cost you?

Before this year's cuts, the city's budget office and the Council's own budget analyst (the IBA) both put the FY2027 gap at $118M. That gap is in the city's main account for everyday services (the General Fund), and it comes back every year until fixed (a structural deficit). Below, it is split per resident and per household, and turned into a sales tax and a monthly fee. The adopted budget then cut spending and also skipped most of the rainy-day deposit its own policy calls for (the reserve contribution), so the "after cuts" view shows two numbers.

Dig deeper

The gap has more than one size because it is measured at different moments and counted in different ways. Before this year's cuts, the city's budget office and the IBA agreed on $118M. After the cuts, the city's budget office reports a net structural deficit of $23.1M. Counted the IBA's way, which adds back the $80.1M reserve deposit the budget skipped, it is $103.2M. Both are real measurements of different things, not a disagreement about arithmetic.

According to KPBS, the IBA also expects a gap of about $30M in FY2028. That is a forecast for next year, so it appears as a marker on the chapter 1 chart instead of here.
