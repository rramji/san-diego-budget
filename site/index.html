San Diego's city budget, explained

This page walks through the City of San Diego's budget for FY2027 (July 2026 to June 2027), which plans about $6.5 billion of spending: where the money comes from, where it goes, and why it falls short. It then looks at what piles up when money is short, how long this has been going on, and what the documents say about five common claims.

What is the city budget, and where does the money come from?

The City of San Diego plans to spend about $6.5 billion in FY2027 (July 2026 to June 2027). Most of that money is walled off by law into separate pots, and each pot can pay only for what its own rules allow: water bills pay for the water system, for example. Only one pot, the General Fund (about $2.3 billion), is general-purpose money that the City Council can spend on any city service.

Why doesn't a wealthy city have more money?

Of each dollar of basic property tax paid in San Diego County, cities get about 18¢ on average, so high home values don't mean a big check for City Hall; schools and the county get most of the rest. The city also keeps just 1 cent of the 7.75% sales tax, and its General Fund gets 5.5 of the 10.5 cents of base hotel tax, with most of the rest set aside for promoting tourism. Meanwhile, the city's costs have been growing faster than its income.

The gap: how big is it, and how was it closed?

Before this year's budget decisions, the city's budget office (the Mayor's Department of Finance) and the Council's independent budget analyst (the IBA) both put the FY2027 gap in the city's main account for everyday services (the General Fund) at $118M. The budget closed it, as the law requires, with cuts, new revenue and other measures, and by again delaying most of the deposit into the rainy-day reserve that the city's own policy calls for (the reserve contribution). What is left comes back next year (a structural deficit), and its size depends on what is counted, so this section gives each size and says what it counts.

Is this debt?

Not in the usual sense. A structural deficit is not money the city owes anyone. It means some ongoing costs are paid for with money that won't come back, like paying rent from savings.

What does pile up is covered in the next section: the rainy-day reserve falls further behind its target, repairs to roads and buildings get put off, and borrowing for building projects (capital borrowing, not borrowing to cover the deficit) adds yearly debt payments. The city's pension promises, which it pays down every year, are covered there too.

What piles up: reserves, infrastructure, pension

When money runs short, some costs aren’t dropped, only put off, and they pile up. Here are three: the city’s rainy-day savings are below its own target, the list of building and repair projects without money keeps growing, and the pension fund holds less than it has promised.

The rainy-day reserve

At the end of FY2026 (June 30, 2026), the city’s rainy-day savings (its General Fund reserves) held $202.4M, by the city’s year-end count, which is not yet audited. Its policy called for $262.9M that year, by the IBA’s calculation.

Roads and buildings

Over the five years FY2027–FY2031, the city’s building and repair needs run $7.82B beyond the money it expects to have: $12.82B of needs against $5.01B of funding, in its five-year plan for capital projects (the CIP Outlook). That is about $1.3B more than a year earlier.

The pension

The city’s pension fund holds about 76 cents for every dollar of retirement benefits its workers have earned so far: it is 76.1% funded, at the June 30, 2025 valuation.

How long has this been going on?

The sections above look at one budget year. This chart looks back to FY2012. In many of those years, the city's regular yearly costs were bigger than its regular yearly income (a structural deficit), and the difference was covered with money that will not come back, such as federal COVID relief, savings, and skipped deposits into the city's reserve fund (one-time resources). Through FY2021, the bars count the one-time money that the Council's independent budget analyst (the IBA) found in each proposed budget. From FY2022 they show the net gap in adopted budgets, which peaked in FY2025 at about $181M, or $204M counting the skipped reserve deposit (the faint top segment of each bar). Years marked n/r were not reported, not zero.
