San Diego's city budget, explained

This page walks through the City of San Diego's budget for FY2027 (July 2026 to June 2027), which plans about $6.5 billion of spending: where the money comes from, where it goes, and why it falls short. It then looks at what piles up when money is short, how long this has been going on, and what the documents say about five common claims.

What is the city budget, and where does the money come from?

The City of San Diego plans to spend about $6.5 billion in FY2027 (July 2026 to June 2027). Most of that money is walled off by law into separate pots, and each pot can pay only for what its own rules allow: water bills pay for the water system, for example. Only one pot, the General Fund (about $2.3 billion), is general-purpose money that the City Council can spend on any city service.

How long has this been going on?

The sections above look at one budget year. This chart looks back to FY2012. In many of those years, the city's regular yearly costs were bigger than its regular yearly income (a structural deficit), and the difference was covered with money that will not come back, such as federal COVID relief, savings, and skipped deposits into the city's reserve fund (one-time resources). Through FY2021, the bars count the one-time money that the Council's independent budget analyst (the IBA) found in each proposed budget. From FY2022 they show the net gap in adopted budgets, which peaked in FY2025 at about $181M, or $204M counting the skipped reserve deposit (the faint top segment of each bar). Years marked n/r were not reported, not zero.

The bars are not all measured the same way. Through FY2021, each bar counts all the one-time money in that year's proposed budget. From FY2022, each bar is what was left after subtracting one-time spending, in the adopted budget. The FY2027 bar comes from the IBA's review of the proposed budget.

The faint segment on top of a bar is the deposit into the city's reserve fund that the budget skipped. The IBA's table shows each year's gap both with and without it; the solid part is the version without it, which measures every year the same way. In FY2012 the IBA's count already included skipped reserve contributions, so that bar is split rather than raised.
