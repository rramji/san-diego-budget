San Diego's budget gap, explained

The city plans to spend about $6.5 billion this year, but only about $2.3 billion of it is the part Council actually votes on (the General Fund). That part does not add up on its own, and the shortfall has a history.

How long has this been going on?

The city has been spending more than it reliably brings in for more than a decade (a structural deficit), and voters turned down a sales-tax increase in 2010 and again in 2024. Year after year the difference was covered with money that will not come back: federal COVID relief, savings, and skipped deposits into the city's reserve fund (one-time resources). The older bars count the one-time money the city's Independent Budget Analyst (IBA) found in each proposed budget, while the recent ones are net structural deficits in adopted budgets, except the latest, which is from the review of the proposed budget. The gap peaked at about $181M in FY2025, and the IBA expects about $30M more in FY2028.

Where is the money, and what can actually be cut?

Most of the city’s money is spoken for before anyone votes. Of the the city plans to spend, only the General Fund is general-purpose money that the City Council decides how to use. The rest is tied by law to specific purposes, such as water service or road projects. Even inside the General Fund, pension and debt payments are contractual, so the four bars below narrow the whole budget down to the part that can actually be cut.

What would closing the gap cost you?

For FY2027 the city's budget office first found that the money coming in would fall $118M short of what the city planned to spend, a recurring shortfall that planners call a structural deficit. That is the main spending account, the General Fund. Below, the same gap is divided up in everyday terms: per resident, per household, as a sales tax, as a monthly fee. The adopted budget then cut the gap, and the cuts are not the only thing that changed it. The budget also skipped almost all of its yearly rainy-day deposit (the reserve contribution), which is why the "after cuts" view shows two numbers.

Dig deeper

The gap has more than one size because it is measured at different moments and counted in different ways. Before this year's cuts, the city's budget office and the Independent Budget Analyst (the City Council's own nonpartisan budget office) agreed on $118M. After the cuts, the city reports a net structural deficit of $23.1M, while the Analyst counts $103.2M, because it adds back the $80.1M reserve deposit the budget skipped. Both are real measurements of different things, not a disagreement about arithmetic. The Analyst also expects a gap of about $30M in FY2028, but that is a forward estimate of a different quantity (next year's structural deficit, reported by KPBS), so it is shown as a marker on the chapter 1 chart and not in this toggle.
