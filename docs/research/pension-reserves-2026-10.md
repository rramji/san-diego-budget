## A1. Latest actuarial valuation (June 30, 2025, which sets the FY 2027 payment)

**Plain English.** Each year an actuary (Cheiron) estimates what the City owes in pensions already earned by current and former employees. That estimate is the *actuarial liability*: $14.51 billion as of June 30, 2025. The actuary then compares it with the money in the pension fund. For this comparison it uses a "smoothed" asset value (the *actuarial value of assets*, $11.05 billion), which spreads one year's market gains or losses over several years. The gap between the two is the *unfunded actuarial liability* (UAL): $3.46 billion. The *funded ratio*, 76.1%, means the fund holds about 76 cents for every dollar of pension promises earned so far. Measured at market value instead of smoothed value, the ratio is 78.0%. The actuary says both ratios are the highest since the 2008 "Great Recession".

All four numbers you had noted are **confirmed**, and **they are City-only**. SDCERS runs three separate plans, each with its own valuation report: the City, the Airport Authority and the Port District. The $3.46B, 76.1% and $563.2M figures belong to the City plan. IBA 26-11 calls the $3.46B the "pension system's" UAL, but the number matches the City-only report. None of the three reports states a single combined SDCERS-wide UAL, and I did not add them up.

## A2. What the numbers mean, and what drove the FY 2027 payment increase

**Plain English.** The *Actuarially Determined Contribution* (ADC) is the City's required pension payment for the year. It has three parts: (1) the *normal cost*, meaning the cost of pension benefits employees earn this year; (2) a payment toward the unfunded liability, spread over a schedule of years like a mortgage; and (3) administrative expenses. For FY 2027 the ADC is $563.2M citywide, up $30.0M (the actuary's figure is $30.1M) from $533.2M. The General Fund pays roughly 70% of it. The rest comes from other City funds, such as water and sewer.

What drove the FY 2027 increase:
1. **Pay raises (the biggest factor).** Salaries rose faster than the actuary had assumed. Higher pay means higher pensions later, so the liability grows. This "liability experience loss" added **$26.4M** to the ADC. Average pay per active member rose 7.4% in one year. The actuary says salary increases above expectations have added more than $600M to the liability over the last four years.
2. **Built-in growth: +$9.2M.** This is the increase that would have happened even if every assumption had been met. It covers expected wage growth and the gradual phase-in of past investment results under the smoothing method.
3. **Prop B police tier conversion: +$2.3M.** Police hired after June 30, 2013 under the Prop B-era "Tier V" benefit were moved back, retroactively, to the older "Tier IV" benefit. The $2.3M covers missed normal-cost payments plus the first installment on the shortfall this created.
4. **Investment gains: −$7.8M.** Returns beat the 6.5% assumption, which partly offset the increases above.
5. **Assumption or method changes: $0.0M** this year. The next experience study, covering data through FY 2025, is expected to affect the FY 2028 ADC.

**Proposition B background.** In June 2012 voters approved Prop B, which closed the pension plan to new hires other than police and put them in a 401(k)-style plan instead. A court invalidated it in early 2021. The City reopened the plan to non-police employees on July 10, 2021 and brought the affected employees back in, with costs that include "Make-Whole Payments". The June 30, 2023 valuation was the first to include these reinstated members. The actuary attributes "the significant increase to the Actuarial Liability in 2023" to them. Over the long run, the largest sources of UAL growth since 2009 have been changes in assumptions (a lower discount rate and longer life expectancies), followed by salary-driven liability losses.

## A3. Payment schedule: payoff date, ADC history, projected ADCs

**Plain English.** The unfunded liability is not one loan but 33 separate "layers", each paid off on its own schedule. A new layer is added each year for that year's gains, losses or rule changes. In January 2019 the SDCERS Board also set a minimum: the City pays at least $275.5M a year toward the UAL until the plan is 100% funded. In the actuary's baseline projection, which assumes every assumption comes true including 6.5% returns every year, the City plan reaches **100% funded at the June 30, 2041 valuation**. The required payment then drops sharply. Real results will differ. A big drop is also projected for **FY 2029**, when some older layers are paid off. Those include the June 30, 2007 UAL layer and a layer of the 2012 non-police UAL, each with 2 years left. The longest-running layer still on the books has 23 years left.

The payment has more than doubled in ten years: about $255M in FY 2016 versus $563M in FY 2027.

## B5. General Fund reserve policy, and the actual balances

**Plain English.** The City keeps two General Fund rainy-day funds, which are added together to measure the reserve:
- **Emergency Reserve**: only for true emergencies such as a natural disaster. Spending it needs a two-thirds Council vote declaring a public emergency. It "will not be accessed to meet operating shortfalls."
- **Stability Reserve**: for unexpected revenue drops or unplanned critical costs. Spending it needs a Council majority. If it is used, the Mayor must present a refill plan within one year.
- **Pension Payment Stabilization Reserve**: meant to cushion year-to-year *increases* in the pension payment. Under the current policy it is funded only **after** the General Fund reserves reach their full 16.7% goal. The FY27 Adopted Budget says it "will not be funded or maintained" until then. No current balance was found.

**Targets.** The long-run goal is 16.7% of the average of the last three years of audited General Fund operating revenue: 8.0% Emergency plus 8.7% Stability, to be reached by **FY 2030**. In December 2022 the Council lowered the interim targets and pushed the deadline from FY 2025 to FY 2030. The FY 2026 target was 14.1%; the FY 2027 target is 14.7%.

**Actual balances.** The total has been frozen at **$207.1M** (Emergency $107.6M, Stability $99.5M) since FY 2023, because contributions were skipped. The FY 2026 draw then took it to **$202.4M**, or 10.9% of revenue against the 14.1% target.

## B6. The FY 2026 $4.7M Stability Reserve draw

**Plain English.** FY 2026 (July 2025 – June 2026) ended $4.7M short. As late as the third quarter the City expected a $1.7M cushion. Then revenues came in $15.4M lower than expected, excluding an accounting entry for a Solid Waste fund asset transfer, while spending came in $9.1M lower. That net $6.4M swing erased the cushion. The single biggest cause was **$7.4M in state disaster reimbursements (CalOES, for the January 2024 storm) that had not arrived by year-end**. The City covered the gap with the Stability Reserve, cutting the GF reserve from $207.1M to $202.4M. Both the IBA and Finance say the City will refill the reserve with the CalOES money if it arrives in FY 2027, consistent with Council resolution R-316815. The $1.7M FY27 reserve deposit in the adopted budget will not happen, because there was no excess equity.

**Is it the first draw?** The IBA calls it "the first time in recent history that the City has drawn from its General Fund reserves" (IBA 26-25 p.12) and "for the first time in recent memory" (p.7). Related history:
- Authority to use the Stability Reserve was also requested at FY 2025's third quarter. A $10.1M deficit was projected then, but it was never used. The IBA says this "appears to be the third time since FY 2008 that reserves use has been requested in the Third Quarter Report" (IBA 26-17 p.14).
- During COVID, the FY 2021 Adopted Budget "maintains the City's reserve levels" and "does not use reserves" (FY 2021 Adopted Budget Highlights).
- I found no definitive all-time history. Phrase it as "the first draw in recent history, per the IBA".

## B7. Skipped FY 2027 reserve contribution ($80.1M): how far below target, and when the target will be met

**Plain English.** The reserve target rises every year, both because the target percentage climbs and because revenues grow. To meet the FY 2027 target of 14.7% ($287.2M), the City would have had to add **$80.1M**. To avoid deeper cuts, the FY 2027 budget skipped that deposit. It budgeted only up to $1.7M from leftover funds, and YE26 now says even that will not happen. Before the FY 2026 draw, reserves were projected at **10.6%** of revenue at the end of FY 2027, against 14.7% required. The City has skipped deposits several times: FY 2021–2022, FY 2024 (waived), FY 2025, FY 2026 and FY 2027.

**When will the target be met?** On paper, the current policy still says 16.7% by FY 2030, but no deposits are planned. The Department of Finance plans to bring a revised policy to the Council by the end of 2026. A preliminary version shown to a Council committee in April 2026 floated pausing deposits in FY 2027 and FY 2028 while keeping reserves near 10%, then depositing 0.5–1.0% of revenue a year. The IBA estimates that at the top 1.0% rate, after two years with no deposits, "it could take around 15 years" to reach 16.7%. No new target date has been adopted.

**Confusing or contradictory points**
