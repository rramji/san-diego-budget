## Big picture: what "deficit" means here

San Diego is legally required to adopt a balanced budget every year, so the "deficit" is a **projected gap**: if the City kept today's services, its regular yearly costs would grow faster than its regular yearly revenue. The City closes the gap each year with cuts, new fees, and one-time money (for example, skipping deposits into its reserve, or "rainy day," fund). The IBA calls this a "structural budget deficit": "when ongoing revenue sources are not enough to support ongoing expenses" (IBA25-36 p.5).

## Q1. Property tax: how much of each dollar reaches City Hall?

**Plain-English answer.** Property tax is the City's biggest revenue source: $881.0 million in FY2027, 39% of the General Fund. But the City gets only a slice of each property-tax dollar. The basic 1% tax is split among the County, school districts, special districts, the leftover redevelopment fund, and cities. Across San Diego County, cities get **about 17.8 cents of every dollar** of the basic 1% tax on average. That is 12.4 cents in regular property tax plus 5.4 cents the state routes to cities through property tax to replace car-registration fee (VLF) money it took away in 2004. Schools get about 44.7 cents.

I did **not find** a primary source that gives the City of San Diego's own share as a single number. The "about 17 cents" figure in circulation is the **countywide average for all 18 cities**, as the City's own budget says ("for every $100 collected the average allocation to cities within San Diego County totals $17.80"). The actual split differs from one neighborhood "tax rate area" to the next. The budget says residual redevelopment money reaches taxing entities "at a rate ranging from 17.0 to 22.0 percent." That range covers only the redevelopment residual, not the whole levy.

**Why high home values don't turn into equally high City revenue (Proposition 13).** Prop 13 was approved by voters in June 1978.
1. It caps the basic tax at **1% of assessed value**.
2. A property's assessed value starts at its **purchase price**. After that it can rise **at most 2% a year**, or by inflation if that is lower, no matter how fast market prices climb.
3. A property is **reassessed to market value only when it sells** (or when new construction is added).

So a home bought decades ago may be taxed on a small fraction of what it would sell for today (LAO p.10). Two more limits:
- The way the 1% is divided among local governments was fixed by state law (AB 8) based on each agency's share in the **late 1970s** (LAO p.20).
- Property tax payments lag the housing market by about **12–18 months** (GFR27 p.63).

The FY2027 budget expects property tax to grow 4.0% over the FY2026 projected base (GFR27 p.66), with the 2% Prop 13 inflation adjustment applied (p.63). The IBA calls that growth "the lowest since FY 2022" for the core pieces (IBA25-36 p.10).

## Q2. Sales tax: the City gets 1 cent of 7.75 cents

**Plain-English answer.** Of the 7.75% sales tax charged in San Diego, the City's General Fund gets **1 cent per dollar** under the state's Bradley-Burns law. The rest goes to the state (3.94%), state-run local programs (1.56% Local Revenue Fund, 0.5% Local Public Safety Fund), county transportation (0.25%), and the regional TransNet transportation tax (0.5%). San Diego has **no city-only add-on sales tax**. It is among the lowest-rate cities in the state. The City's 2024 ballot materials said the rate was "tied for the fourth lowest out of the 482 individual cities in the State."

By contrast, as of Oct 1, 2026, CDTFA lists:
- Los Angeles: 10.25%
- San Jose: 10.0%
- Sacramento: 8.75%
- Oakland: 10.75%
- San Francisco: 8.625%

FY2027 GF sales tax revenue is $386.7 million (17.1% of GF revenue).

**Measure E (Nov 5, 2024)**, a 1-cent general sales tax that would have raised the rate to 8.75%, **failed 49.69% Yes to 50.31% No**: 284,940 yes to 288,446 no, a margin of 3,506 votes (CALC: 288,446 − 284,940). The City estimated it would have raised **about $400 million a year** for the General Fund. The IBA later put a future 1-cent increase at "$360 million to $400 million" a year.

## Q3. Hotel tax (Transient Occupancy Tax, TOT)

**Plain-English answer.** Hotel guests pay a base hotel tax of **10.5%**. By City law, only **5.5 of those 10.5 cents** go straight into the General Fund: **$169.7 million in FY2027**, just 7.5% of GF revenue. The other cents are split:
- **4.0 cents** go to "Special Promotional Programs," meaning tourism promotion, arts, and visitor-related facilities ($122.5M).
- **1.0 cent** goes to whatever the Council directs ($30.6M).

Some of that 5 cents flows back to the General Fund as reimbursements for eligible visitor-related costs, such as safety and park maintenance. In FY2027 the 1-cent Council share is being transferred to the General Fund because of the deficit.

On top of the base rate, voter-approved **Measure C (March 2020, not 2024)** raised the total hotel tax to **11.75%, 12.75%, or 13.75%** depending on location, collected since **May 1, 2025**. Measure C money is **restricted**:
- Through Oct 31, 2030: 59% to the Convention Center and 41% to homelessness.
- From Nov 1, 2030: 59% Convention Center, 31% homelessness, 10% street repair.

Measure C money cannot be used for general services, although its homelessness share does replace spending the General Fund would otherwise face. Separately, larger hotels pay a **Tourism Marketing District** assessment. That is not TOT and does not go to the General Fund.

Why so little: tourism money shows up mostly in TOT and some sales tax, but most TOT is earmarked by law. The budget also expects only 2.1% TOT growth for FY2027, with international travel down in 2025 (GFR27 p.71–72).

## Q4. Free trash pickup (People's Ordinance) and the new fee

**Plain-English answer.** A 1919 voter-approved law, the "People's Ordinance," required the City to pick up trash for free from homes on public streets. In practice that meant most single-family homes. Apartment dwellers and businesses paid private haulers. The cost came out of the General Fund, competing with police, fire, and roads. In November 2022 voters approved **Measure B**, which allowed a fee but did not set one. The Council adopted fees on **June 9, 2025**, starting July 1, 2025 (FY2026). The standard 95-gallon three-bin service costs **$43.60 a month ($523.20 a year)**, billed on the county property-tax bill.

Measured three ways, free trash pickup cost the General Fund:
- **$58.7 million** in the FY2023 budget (IBA, 2022)
- **$80.8 million and 181 positions** shifted off the General Fund in FY2026
- **about $128.8 million a year**, the full revenue requirement in the 2025 cost study, which includes service improvements

The fee does not pay for police or parks directly. It **frees General Fund money** by moving the trash bill elsewhere (IBA26-11 p.3, fn.4).

**Twist (2026):** Under a settlement, the Council voted June 8, 2026 to **cut fees starting July 1, 2027**. The 95-gallon bundle drops from a previously adopted $55 to $38.75. The City says this means "subsidies from the City's General Fund" will be needed again, but **the dollar amount of that future subsidy was not found.**

## Q5. Costs: why spending grows faster than revenue

**Plain-English answer.** The IBA projects the City's regular revenue will grow **about 3.2% a year** in FY2028–2031, after just **1.4%** in FY2027. Over the prior five years (FY2021–2025), local inflation averaged **6.2% a year**. Note that these two numbers cover different periods. Meanwhile the Outlook's FY2027 baseline costs grow **5.4% ($116.9M)** just to keep services as they are. The biggest drivers:
- **People.** Wages are assumed to rise 2.94% ($29.2M). Workers' comp is up $9.9M and the pension payment up $4.9M. Each 1% raise costs about $9.9M.
- **Paying for infrastructure.** Bond payments for capital projects are up $18.6M.
- **Vehicles.** Fleet fees are up $17.1M.
- **Contracts and supplies** are assumed to grow 3.2%.

**Pensions are a big fixed cost.** The City's pension payment (the actuarially determined contribution, or ADC) from the General Fund is **$396.3M in FY2027**, up $18.2M, partly because salaries rose faster than the actuaries assumed. Pension plus retiree health is about **$413.5M** (IBA projection).

**Homelessness:** $103.0M in FY2027 spending across funds. The IBA projects a funding gap of $15.0M (FY2027) rising to $27.9M (FY2031) as state grants (HHAP) shrink.

**Personnel overall** (wages plus benefits) is $1.59 billion of the $2.26 billion General Fund (CALC: 1,590.6 / 2,256.6 = 70.5%; both figures from GFE27 p.82).

The IBA also notes the City has deferred pay increases and maintenance for years. Recent contracts have raised pay toward regional medians (IBA26-11 p.77).

## Q6. Per-capita revenue or tax burden vs peer California cities

**Plain-English answer.** I found **no current primary source that states San Diego's per-capita General Fund revenue next to peer California cities**. The IBA says qualitatively (April 2026) that San Diego's "major General Fund revenues, in comparison to other peer cities, are relatively low." The most recent explicit comparison I found is old. IBA 06-10 (2006) summarized a 2005 Center for Policy Initiatives report that found San Diego "generates lower revenue per capita than the average of the ten largest cities in California, due in large part to tax rates that fall below average." The best current, sourced comparison is **tax rates**: sales tax (Q2 above) and the 2024 ballot language ("tied for the fourth lowest" of 482 cities).

## Suggested one-paragraph synthesis for the explainer (all claims sourced above)

San Diego's homes are expensive, but the City sees only a slice of property tax. Across the county, cities get about 18 cents of each dollar of the basic 1% tax (GFR27 p.62). Prop 13 also limits taxable value growth to 2% a year until a home sells (LAO p.10). The City keeps 1 cent of the 7.75% sales tax, one of the lowest rates in California (GFR27 p.67; MEASE p.1), and voters narrowly rejected a second cent in 2024, 49.69% to 50.31% (ROV p.12). Tourists' hotel tax is mostly earmarked: only 5.5 of the 10.5 base cents go straight to the General Fund ($169.7M), and Measure C's extra hotel tax is restricted to the Convention Center and homelessness (GFR27 p.70; CBO27 p.49). Meanwhile costs keep climbing. Baseline spending grows 5.4% a year (IBA25-36 p.14) while revenue is projected to grow about 3.2% (IBA25-36 p.9), driven by wages, pensions ($396.3M ADC; GFE27 p.85), debt for long-neglected infrastructure, and homelessness. A century-old free trash service ate $80.8M a year of the General Fund until a 2025 fee moved it off the books (ES26 p.6), and a 2026 settlement will partly reverse that from July 2027 (ESDREL).
